Projectile Dynamics — Newton's Second Law

Kinematic and dynamic analysis of a 50 g projectile moving in the gravitational field with non-negligible air resistance. The practical reproduces the USTHB scale-1/80 recording and the complete vector/force workflow used to identify the drag law.

Official problem

A point-like object of mass m = 50 g is launched from O in the vertical xOy plane with initial velocity V₀ at angle θ. Its positions are supplied every 0.1 s on a document at scale 1/80. Air resistance is significant.

P⃗ + f⃗ = m a⃗

The study interval is Δtₑ = 0.6 s. The final objective is to determine the mathematical form of the air-drag force from a graph of |f| versus |V|.

Work required by the USTHB handout

  1. Construct successive displacement vectors Δrᵢ over 0.6 s.
  2. Use the corresponding mean-velocity vectors to construct ΔV at P₁–P₄.
  3. Measure ΔV graphically, convert to m/s, then calculate |a|.
  4. At P₂, draw a₂ at 1 cm ↔ 5 m/s², determine aₙ₂ and V₂, calculate ρ₂ = V₂²/aₙ₂, mark curvature centre O₂ and compare a circle with the local trajectory.
  5. Calculate |ma|, draw ma at 1 cm ↔ 0.1 N, then construct f = ma − P graphically.
  6. Determine V₁–V₄ and plot |f| versus |V|.
  7. Infer the drag-force law and its direction relative to V.

Theoretical background

Projectile motion with air resistance obeys Newton's second law m a = m g + f. Unlike the vacuum case, the acceleration is not constant because the drag force depends on the instantaneous velocity. In intrinsic coordinates, the tangential component changes the speed and the normal component bends the trajectory.

The local radius of curvature satisfies ρ=v²/aₙ. Once a is obtained from successive velocity vectors, the drag is reconstructed experimentally from f=m a−m g. Its direction should oppose the motion; plotting |f| against |v| then allows the data to reveal whether the magnitude is closer to a linear or quadratic speed law.
