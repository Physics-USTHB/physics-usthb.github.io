Calorimetry

Official USTHB calorimetry practical: test energy conservation with hot/cold water in three trials, then determine the specific heat of a metal sample.

3 water-mixing trialsenergy conservationboiling-water metal heatingspecific heat

Part 1 — conservation of energy

Use two calorimeters, hot water, cold water, a balance and a thermometer. Perform three trials with different masses and temperatures. In Trial 1 add hot water to cold water; in Trials 2 and 3 reverse the order and add cold water to hot water while stirring continuously.

ΔQ = m c ΔT
ΔQcold + ΔQhot ≈ 0

Part 2 — metal specific heat

Weigh the metal, heat it in boiling water for about 10 minutes without letting it touch the beaker bottom, measure atmospheric pressure to determine the actual boiling temperature T₀, then transfer the hot metal into a known mass of water and measure equilibrium.

M c′(Tf−T₀) + m cwater(Tf−Ti) = 0

Use cwater=4180 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹.

Theoretical background

When bodies at different temperatures are placed in an insulated calorimeter, they approach a common equilibrium temperature while total energy is conserved. For each body without phase change, Q=mc(Tf−Ti); heat lost by the hotter bodies is balanced by heat gained by the colder bodies and the calorimeter.

A metal's specific heat is obtained by heating a known mass to a known initial temperature and then mixing it with water. Because the heating bath is boiling water, the initial metal temperature is the local boiling temperature rather than automatically 100°C; atmospheric pressure therefore matters when the handout asks for a pressure correction.
